SAMBANDHA - ML & SIMULATION PITCH
==================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
30-SECOND ELEVATOR PITCH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Sambandha is a civic platform where Nepali citizens don't just read policy
drafts — they *pressure-test* them. When a district official drops a draft
budget, citizens comment and vote, and our simulation engine answers the
question every Nepali wants answered: "What does this actually do for us?"
It projects jobs, efficiency and completion risk from a real ledger of 224
provincial projects, and — for health policy — predicts success probability,
coverage gains per crore, and insurance claim volumes, using models trained
in Python with scikit-learn. Every number on screen traces back to a
published source: the MoF Red Book FY 2080/81, NDHS 2022, NHIF and Census
2021. We call it a calibrated simulation — anchored in real public data,
surfaced with its assumptions, so citizens argue about policy, not about
the math.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3-MINUTE DEMO NARRATIVE (what the judges see)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. THE DRAFT
   "Here's a provincial health insurance expansion draft sitting in our
   portal — already reviewed by an expert and two district officers, with
   citizens from 5 districts having weighed in. We simulated it as-is."

2. THE CONSENSUS LAYER (civic)
   Show comments with role badges and Nepali/English sentiment; the
   approve/disapprove votes; the "what your neighbors think" district map.
   One line: "Citizens tell us the DEMAND side — is this draft politically
   and socially viable?"

3. THE ML LAYER (evidence)
   Run the draft through the simulator — "Now the SUPPLY side — can we
   deliver?"
   - Policy success probability from the logistic regression (trained on
     198 policy outcome records, 90% accuracy, 0.889 AUC)
   - Per-program coverage gains per crore (per-program linear regressions,
     R² 0.51)
   - Claims forecast from the citizen profile (400 records, R² 0.957 — a
     citizen in this district filing ~NPR 121,000 in claims, MAE ~NPR 15k)

4. THE PROJECTION ENGINE
   Nearest-project matches pulled live from the 224-project provincial
   ledger — jobs, efficiency, completion, overrun risk, with a confidence
   score. Highlight the match table: "These are real projects from the
   ledger that look like your policy — the engine averages their outcomes,
   weighted by how similar they are."

5. THE HONESTY BEAT (say this deliberately)
   "These rows are deterministic synthetic records calibrated to published
   data — we don't pretend we trained on real outcome data, because Nepal
   doesn't publish outcome data at this granularity. That's why every
   screen reports the engine, sample size, and sources."

6. THE ENGINEERING BEAT
   Kill the ML service live (or mention it): the app doesn't break — it
   falls back to in-process reference models, and every response is tagged
   "engine: python" vs "engine: js-fallback". Model metadata (accuracy,
   AUC, R², trained-at) is served to the UI.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE HONEST FRAMING (use this verbatim, it's your strongest move)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"We trained on deterministic, synthetic records calibrated to real public
statistics — the anchors are real (Red Book, NDHS, NHIF, Census), the rows
are generated, and the UI says so. We're demonstrating the pipeline: how a
government could train this on real outcome data tomorrow, with the same
code. The honest claim is 'calibrated simulation,' not 'prediction.'"

Judges will respect this more than any faked accuracy number.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTIONS TO WATCH OUT FOR — WITH THE ANSWERS THAT LAND
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. Your data is synthetic — why should I believe any of it?
A1. The anchors are real and citable. Every generated record is a
    deterministic function of published statistics (Red Book provincial
    budgets, NDHS coverage rates, NHIF claim patterns), and the UI
    attributes every estimate to its source. We're building the pipeline
    governments need; the calibration is the honest step we can take today.
    Swap the generator for a real outcome dataset and the same code trains
    the same models.

Q2. What is actually machine learning here vs. statistics?
A2. Three trained models: logistic regression trained with gradient descent
    (success probability), per-program linear regression (impact), and a
    claims regression (forecast). Plus a distance-weighted k-NN projection
    engine over the project ledger. The honest breakdown: k-NN and linear
    models are simple — deliberately, because a district official should be
    able to explain them. What's REAL ML is the training pipeline,
    validation, and metadata — not hand-waving.

Q3. Why do you have the same models in JavaScript and Python? Is Python
    cosmetic?
A3. Python is the primary engine; the JS reference models exist as a
    reliability layer so the app never dies when the ML service is down —
    we demoed the fallback live. Also: the models are trained on exactly
    the same records in both, and we verified byte-identical outputs (same
    jobs, same confidence, same probabilities). Python isn't a logo on a
    slide — the sklearn training, joblib artifacts and REST endpoints are
    the actual running system.

Q4. R² = 0.51 on budget impact — that's weak. Why?
A4. It's the honest number for a hard problem: predicting province-level
    health coverage outcomes from budget alone. The claims model
    (R² 0.957) shows the pipeline can fit tight when the signal is strong.
    0.51 tells the citizen something real — budget explains about half the
    variance; the rest is implementation. We surface it instead of hiding
    it.

Q5. How is the confidence score (20–96) computed? Is it statistical?
A5. It's a similarity score: distance between the input and the 6 nearest
    ledger matches in budget/province space, converted to a confidence.
    It is NOT a statistical confidence interval — we say that explicitly
    in the UI. It measures "how well do historical precedents cover this
    scenario?" — that's the honest claim.

Q6. How does sentiment analysis work?
A6. A transparent rule-based lexicon (English + Nepali, including a
    bad-word list) with per-comment majority voting — not a black-box
    model. For a hackathon demo it's fast and explainable; a production
    version would fine-tune on Nepali corpora. Honesty over opacity.

Q7. Can this actually predict? Won't this mislead citizens?
A7. We never say "this will happen." Every output is labeled simulation
    with sources and sample sizes; we show assumptions and the matched
    precedents so a citizen can see the reasoning chain. If anything, it
    makes policy debate better — citizens argue with evidence on screen,
    not rumors.

Q8. What happens at scale / in production?
A8. The ML service is stateless behind an API — it scales horizontally;
    artifacts persist to disk so it doesn't retrain on every boot;
    retraining is triggered when the dataset version changes. The app was
    built to survive the ML service being down — a production-grade
    assumption most hackathons skip.

Q9. Privacy? Citizens submit comments and votes.
A9. Comments are public by design (it's a civic forum). Votes are
    phone-unique per sector, votes are upserts (one per sector), and auth
    uses httpOnly cookies — no tokens in localStorage. We'd add anonymized
    hashing of phone numbers before any real deployment.

Q10. What would you do with another 6 months?
A10. Real outcome datasets from DHIS2/NHSS-IP, a Nepali-language fine-tuned
     sentiment model, time-series claims forecasting, uncertainty bounds
     from proper statistical methods, and a peer-review round where
     officials can flag incorrect projections in-platform.

Q11. What does the citizen actually DO with this?
A11. They can run the simulator themselves on the draft page, change
     budget, see jobs/confidence change, see their district's stance, and
     their voice enters the official review flow. The transparency
     dashboard (tracking page) shows the whole capital plan — 224 projects,
     status by province, delayed projects — so citizens can audit the
     budget their government already spends.

Q12. Is it Nepali?
A12. The interface is fully bilingual (English/Nepali toggle, Noto Sans
     Devanagari), sentiment handles Nepali text, and all sectors/provinces
     use Nepali naming conventions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FOUR THINGS NOT TO SAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Don't call it "prediction" — call it "projection/simulation"
   (judges will test this).
2. Don't claim real outcome data — own the synthetic-calibrated framing
   FIRST.
3. Don't say "our AI" — name the models (logistic regression, linear
   regression, k-NN).
4. Don't oversell R² 0.51 — say it with the claims model's 0.957 for
   contrast.
